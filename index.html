ICan we trust this data before we ask it anything?

Before any pattern means something, know what's actually here.

A biased, unevenly measured sample of extreme-tolerant life on Earth — not a census, and not yet evidence of anything beyond Earth.

I.1

The raw material

ThermoBase curates 1,238 organisms across 337 structured fields — physiology, environment, metabolism, chemistry, taxonomy, and quality control. Before anything else, this is a sample, shaped by what's been published and characterized, not a random cross-section of life.

Two environment types account for over a third of records. Hot springs and hydrothermal vents dominate; this reflects where researchers have gone looking, not where extremophile life is most common.
I.2

What we don't know

Missing data isn't a footnote — it decides which questions this dataset can even attempt to answer.

Oxygen requirement is unreported for nearly half the database — a larger share than any actual oxygen category. Separately, optimum pressure is missing in 97% of records: pressure is essentially unmeasured across this entire release.
I.3

The evidence gate

Before this data gets to say anything about biosignatures on other worlds, it has to pass its own test for how carefully it talks about biosignatures.

Only 39 of 1,238 records carry any candidate biosignature status at all — and most of those are explicitly flagged high false-positive-risk. That's the caution this entire piece has to earn the right to set aside, and only in the one place it's actually justified.

II.1

Where does this life actually live?

Every downstream pattern inherits whatever shape this measured niche has.

Dense regions are where evidence is thick, not where life is common. Bacteria and Archaea dominate the measured space; Eukaryota are a small minority of this curated release.
II.2

A mention is not evidence

A chemical name appearing in the literature is not the same as adjudicated proof that an organism uses it metabolically.

Only 1 in 5 records reach high-confidence chemical adjudication. Most either have no extracted compound at all, or their chemical context remains explicitly unresolved rather than promoted into a claim.
II.3 — the climax

The stress test

Six metabolic pathways looked predictable from environment alone under ordinary random cross-validation. Only one of them was tested against something harder: could the model still tell pathways apart once it could no longer lean on which branch of the tree of life an organism belonged to?

Random cross-validation shuffles all 1,238 records together, so close relatives can end up on both sides of the split — the model can partly succeed by recognizing lineage, not environment. Grouped validation holds an entire phylum out at once, so the model must generalize to organisms unlike anything it trained on.
